# GPU contention literature and the AI4Research gap

This note distinguishes established sharing/scheduling results from the narrower opportunity of predicting which hardware resources a new AI4Research task or process will stress. It is exploratory evidence, not a product requirement or validated planner design.

## Useful primary sources

| Paper | What it establishes | Reuse and limitation for AI4Research |
|---|---|---|
| [Gandiva: Introspective Cluster Scheduling for Deep Learning](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/xiao), OSDI 2018 | Deep-learning mini-batch iterations are predictable enough to time-slice and migrate jobs; reports improved utilization and faster hyperparameter search. | Reuse the insight that repeated phases enable profiling and scheduling. It optimizes training-job placement and responsiveness; it does not infer low-level component demand for arbitrary research processes. |
| [Salus: Fine-Grained GPU Sharing Primitives for Deep Learning Applications](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2020/file/d9cd83bc91b8c36a0c7c0fcca59228f2-Paper.pdf), MLSys 2020 | Implements fast job switching and dynamic GPU-memory sharing; evaluates packing and scheduling policies for TensorFlow DL jobs. Explicitly discusses unpredictable co-location interference and memory fragmentation. | Reuse memory-capacity/peak tracking and sharing as a scheduling choice. Its mechanism and workload set are framework/DL-specific; memory sharing is not a model of SM, cache, DRAM, CPU, copy-engine, or codec contention. |
| [Gavel: Heterogeneity-Aware Cluster Scheduling Policies for Deep Learning Workloads](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/narayanan-deepak), OSDI 2020 | Uses effective throughput to schedule across accelerator types and sharing configurations; shows heterogeneity-aware allocation improves cluster objectives. | Reuse per-workload/per-device throughput measurements as input to planning. It assumes measured effective throughput; it does not derive resource attribution or safely generalize a contention profile to an unseen task/device. |
| [Serving DNNs like Clockwork](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/gujarati), OSDI 2020 | Shows predictable inference execution can support request-level scheduling, isolation, and latency SLOs across a large model-serving deployment. | Reuse execution-time predictability and admission/batching controls. It is chiefly a serving-system result, with controlled inference execution; it does not predict arbitrary mixed-process hardware pressure or architectural bottlenecks. |
| [Orion: Interference-Aware, Fine-Grained GPU Sharing for ML Applications](https://anakli.inf.ethz.ch/papers/orion_eurosys24.pdf), EuroSys 2024 | Profiles individual kernels and aligns kernel traces with compute-throughput and memory-bandwidth profiles. Shows bursty, complementary compute/memory phases can be profitably co-located; also characterizes interference and scheduling. | Closest precedent for phase/resource feature vectors: preserve phase boundaries and per-kernel resource mix, not one job-wide average. Evaluation is V100-era DNN workloads, and the paper explicitly excludes LLMs as its target sharing workload due to their capacity needs; transfer to varied research tools or other devices requires fresh measurement. |
| [Understanding GPU Resource Interference One Level Deeper](https://anakli.inf.ethz.ch/papers/gpu_interference_socc25.pdf), SoCC 2025 | Characterizes interference through block placement, intra-SM resources, compute pipelines, cache and memory paths; proposes measuring sensitivity with profiling tools and configurable microbenchmarks. Warns the same workload can behave differently across architectures and should be re-profiled. | Reuse resource-specific sensitivity experiments and microbenchmarks; directly supports component-level attribution as a research direction. It is a characterization methodology, not a universal predictor, and notes limited profiler visibility during co-location and closed hardware mechanisms. |

Related SoC evidence: [BWLOCK++: Protecting Real-Time GPU Kernels on Integrated CPU-GPU SoC Platforms](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.08738) reports large GPU slowdowns under memory-intensive CPU co-runners on an integrated Tegra K1 and proposes memory-bandwidth regulation. This makes CPU activity a first-class co-runner on shared-memory systems, while its platform and control mechanism are specific to that SoC generation.

## What is already well established

GPU sharing, time slicing, model-serving batching/admission, accelerator heterogeneity, and the usefulness of profiling known jobs are established topics. A proposal framed only as “profile workloads and schedule to reduce interference” would duplicate substantial prior work. Likewise, a single compute-bound versus memory-bound label is too coarse: complementary average utilization can hide phase overlap, shared-cache or bandwidth interference, and asymmetric slowdown.

## Potential AI4Research contribution

The sharper question is whether history from task/capsule processes can produce a transferable, uncertainty-aware feature representation that predicts a *resource vector over phases* for a new task/process, then use that prediction to simulate feasible workload mixes and guide chip design. Candidate dimensions include SM/compute pipelines, occupancy/register/shared-memory pressure, cache and DRAM bandwidth, memory capacity and paging, CPU demand, host-device/copy traffic, and dedicated engines. Retain process/task identity, phase transitions, batch/input size, runtime/backend, cold/warm state, and device identity as context. Separate a task's solo resource demand from its measured sensitivity to and impact on co-runners; do not collapse asymmetric interference into a single utilization score.

The novelty test should include genuinely held-out processes/workflows and hardware, with a direct comparison against simple baselines (task identity, average SM utilization, compute/memory labels, and measured solo throughput). Evaluate resource-pressure prediction and contention/slowdown prediction separately, retain uncertainty and failure/censored runs, and test whether predicted profiles improve mix simulation or design decisions. Offline replay/profile data can seed models, but online co-run measurements are needed to validate interaction effects.

Two likely gaps require explicit experiments. First, “unified memory” does not identify one uniform bottleneck: discrete-GPU managed-memory migration, CPU-GPU links, integrated SoC shared DRAM, and coherent CPU/GPU memory are different paths. Measure placement, page migration/faults, copy/CPU traffic, and shared-bandwidth contention on each target class instead of transferring a bandwidth label. Second, video encode/decode may use dedicated fixed-function engines (e.g. NVENC/NVDEC) alongside CUDA and memory paths; CUDA-only profiling can miss engine occupancy, supported codec/session limits, or frame-buffer traffic. Track encode and decode separately and verify the actual backend/engine in use, including software fallback. These paths may overlap compute while still contending for memory and data movement, so “independent engine” does not mean “no interference.”

## Provenance and limits

All six main sources above are published primary conference papers with publisher/conference pages or author-hosted papers. The papers support the claims in their rows; the proposed feature-vector transfer, held-out evaluation, unified-memory matrix, and fixed-function media tests are research recommendations inferred from their scope and omissions. Results should be treated as architecture-, driver-, runtime-, workload-, and configuration-specific until independently measured.
