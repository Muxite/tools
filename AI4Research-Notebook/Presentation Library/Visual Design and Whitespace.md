# Visual design, density, and whitespace

## Make each slide useful at a glance

Give the slide a clear focal point and a readable order: takeaway, visual or evidence, then small supporting details. Align related elements and use consistent spacing, color, and type. Keep citations readable. A viewer should know where to look first and what the figure shows.

Do not leave large areas of the canvas unused when the evidence or explanation could use the space. Enlarge the central figure; show a relevant comparison; add labels, a small workflow, or a concise takeaway. When a slide still needs empty space to remain clear, keep it. The aim is purposeful use of space, not filling every corner.

When the slide feels crowded, remove repetitions, simplify the diagram, split the argument, or move supporting detail into notes. Do not shrink a crowded slide until its evidence becomes hard to read. The project’s September deck plan recommends splitting dense slides, with illustrative minimums of 20 pt body, 16 pt tables, and 14 pt excerpts. Treat those as that deck’s documented choices, not a universal accessibility standard.

## Pick a visual form that fits the claim

- Use a diagram for structure, trust boundaries, and responsibilities.
- Use a plot for quantitative comparisons; label axes and include units.
- Use a compact table when readers must compare exact values or alternatives.
- Use a timeline or numbered path for sequences.
- Use code or contract excerpts sparingly, with the relevant portion highlighted.

Keep the same visual encoding stable. Do not rely on color alone to distinguish key categories. Prefer editable, precise charts and diagrams when the audience may need to reuse the deck.

## Review the canvas in context

Check a contact sheet to see rhythm and repetition, then inspect each slide at presentation size. Review the actual PowerPoint output; a source spec can look orderly while the rendered content overflows or becomes too small. Check the projector/display format, contrast, line wrapping, image resolution, labels, footnotes, and citation legibility.

The practical quality check is not “is the slide full?” Ask whether every large gap is intentional, whether the important material is large enough, and whether each visual helps the audience follow the argument.
